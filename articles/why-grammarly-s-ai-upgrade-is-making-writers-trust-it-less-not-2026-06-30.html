Why Grammarly's AI Upgrade Is Making Writers Trust It Less, Not More

June 30, 2026 7 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

A proofreading service charges $200 to polish a 5,000-word report. With Grammarly Premium, it costs $12 a month and takes 15 minutes. That value proposition made Grammarly essential for millions of writers. But in March 2026, when the company rolled out its "GrammarlyGo AI Advanced" upgrade, something unexpected happened: power users started turning features off. Reddit threads filled with writers complaining that their trusted editing assistant had become an overconfident intern who wouldn't stop talking. Facebook groups dedicated to freelance writing saw members canceling subscriptions they'd held for years. The tool that promised to make writing better was, according to a growing chorus of professional writers, making their work feel less like theirs.

This isn't about a buggy release or a price increase. It's about something more fundamental: when an AI writing tool becomes too aggressive in its suggestions, does it cross the line from assistant to interference? And when a company built its reputation on correcting grammar but now wants to generate entire paragraphs, can users still trust it to respect their voice?

What Changed in the March 2026 Update

Grammarly's March update introduced what the company called "contextual generation" — the ability to not just suggest edits but to write new content based on your document's context. Open a blank page, type a sentence fragment, and GrammarlyGo AI Advanced would offer to complete your thought with three paragraphs of perfectly grammatical prose. Working on a conclusion? It would draft one. Need a transition? Consider it done.

The feature launched with the Premium tier at no additional cost, positioning it as added value for existing subscribers. The company's blog post emphasized "amplifying your unique voice" and "eliminating writer's block." The interface changed too: suggestions became more prominent, appearing as full overlays rather than subtle underlines. A new sidebar offered real-time alternatives for entire sentences, not just individual words.

For some users, this was exactly what they wanted. Sales teams writing repetitive outreach emails found it useful. Customer support agents answering common questions appreciated the speed. But for writers whose work depended on authentic voice — journalists, novelists, essayists, content creators — the update felt like an invasion. The tool they'd used to catch typos was now trying to write for them.

By April, the backlash was measurable. A survey by the Professional Writers Alliance found that 43% of members had either downgraded from Premium or disabled AI suggestion features. Chrome Web Store reviews dropped from 4.6 to 4.1 stars in six weeks. Twitter threads from verified journalists showed screenshots of Grammarly suggesting bland corporate speak to replace their carefully crafted prose. One viral post from a romance novelist showed Grammarly trying to rewrite an intimate scene into something that sounded like a technical manual.

The Trust Problem: When Your Editor Becomes a Co-Writer

The concerns center on three core issues, and after examining each, they appear more valid than dismissible.

First: voice dilution. Professional writers spend years developing a distinctive style. Grammarly's original promise was to preserve that style while fixing errors. The new AI features, however, push toward homogenization. The suggested rewrites tend toward safe, corporate, middle-of-the-road language. When you accept enough of these suggestions, your writing starts sounding like everyone else's writing — which is to say, it sounds like the averaged-out internet text the AI was trained on. Content creator Sarah Chen, who writes for Beehiiv newsletters with 50,000+ subscribers, told her audience she stopped using Grammarly Premium after it suggested replacing "I freaked out" with "I experienced significant concern." That's not editing. That's rewriting personality into oblivion.

Second: the erosion of skill. When Grammarly corrected your comma splices, you learned where commas go. When it suggests entire paragraphs, what are you learning? Several writing instructors reported students submitting work that was technically flawless but obviously not written in their voice. The line between "editing assistance" and "AI-written with human approval" gets dangerously blurry. Tools like NeuronWriter focus on SEO optimization while letting writers maintain full control of their prose. Grammarly's approach feels more invasive — like it wants to do the writing itself.

Third: the attribution anxiety. If Grammarly generates three sentences and you tweak two of them, who wrote that paragraph? For academic writers, journalists with editorial standards, or anyone who signs their name to their work, this isn't a philosophical question. It's a professional liability. The tool doesn't mark AI-generated suggestions differently from grammar corrections, so writers found themselves second-guessing which parts of their document were genuinely theirs. One freelance journalist described spending 20 minutes reverse-engineering her own article to figure out which transitions she'd written versus which Grammarly had inserted.

These aren't fringe concerns from technophobes. These are working writers whose livelihoods depend on authentic, trustworthy output questioning whether their most-used tool still serves them.

Grammarly's Defense: Expanding Beyond Grammar

To be fair, Grammarly has been transparent about its evolution. The company's CEO stated in an April interview that the grammar correction market is "solved" and that growth means helping users with broader communication challenges. Their data shows that 68% of users who enable AI generation features continue using them after 30 days, suggesting many people do want this functionality.

Grammarly also points out that every AI feature can be disabled. Users who want only grammar checking can turn off tone suggestions, rewriting recommendations, and generative features entirely. The company argues they're providing options, not forcing anyone to use capabilities they don't want. They've also emphasized that their AI is designed to match your existing writing style by analyzing your document history — though many users report this working inconsistently at best.

The company frames the update as meeting users where the market is heading. With ChatGPT, Wordtune, and Copy.ai already offering generative writing features, Grammarly's position is that staying relevant means expanding capabilities. Their argument: writers who want only proofreading can have that, while those who need drafting help can access it in the same tool.

What This Actually Means for Your Writing Workflow

If you're a Grammarly user wondering whether to adjust your approach, here's what matters most: understand what you're actually using the tool for, then configure it accordingly.

For professional writers, content creators, journalists, or anyone whose byline matters: disable the generative features immediately. Go to Settings > AI Features and turn off "AI writing suggestions" and "generative content." Keep only grammar, spelling, and plagiarism checking active. You'll get the functionality that made Grammarly valuable in the first place without the voice-dilution risk. This takes three minutes and eliminates 90% of the problems described above.

For business users writing emails, reports, and internal documentation: the features might genuinely help. If you're drafting your fifteenth nearly-identical client update this week, having Grammarly generate a template isn't cheating — it's efficiency. Just read every suggestion carefully before accepting. The AI occasionally produces awkward phrasing or makes assumptions about context that aren't accurate.

For students and academics: proceed with extreme caution. Most institutions have policies about AI-assisted writing that haven't caught up to tools like this. Using Grammarly for grammar correction is typically fine. Having it generate paragraphs might violate academic integrity policies. When in doubt, check with your instructor or institution before using generative features on submitted work.

The critical lesson: don't accept suggestions blindly. Grammarly's interface makes it very easy to click "Accept All" and move on. That habit was relatively safe when suggestions were typo fixes. With generative content, that habit can fundamentally alter your writing without you noticing. Treat every AI-generated sentence as a draft that needs your approval, not as authoritative output.

Part of a Bigger Shift in How AI Tools Position Themselves

Grammarly's situation reflects a broader tension in the AI tools market. Companies that built their reputations on narrow, well-defined use cases are now trying to expand into generative AI — and not all of their existing users want that evolution.

Look at similar patterns elsewhere. Fireflies.ai started as a meeting transcription service and recently added "AI meeting summaries" that some users find less accurate than reading the transcript themselves. Otter.ai introduced automated action item extraction that occasionally invents tasks no one actually mentioned. These aren't bad features in themselves, but they represent a shift from "capture what happened" to "interpret and extend what happened" — and that shift changes the trust equation.

The common thread: tools that succeeded by augmenting human capabilities are trying to become tools that replace human effort. That's fine when users opt in knowingly. It becomes problematic when it's presented as an automatic upgrade to something they were already paying for. Grammarly isn't the first tool to face this backlash, and it won't be the last.

The market is also showing that writer-focused users are willing to seek alternatives when tools over-rotate toward AI generation. Platforms like ElevenLabs focus specifically on voice and audio, leaving text entirely to the user. Automation tools like Make.com let users build exactly the workflow they want without imposing AI features they don't need. There's a counter-trend emerging: tools that do less but do it exactly right, versus tools that try to do everything and risk doing nothing particularly well.

Worth Considering: Alternatives

ProWritingAid: Focuses heavily on style and grammar without aggressive generative features; particularly good for long-form writers and novelists who want detailed analysis without AI rewriting.

Hemingway Editor: A simpler tool that highlights complex sentences and common writing issues; no AI generation, just clear feedback on readability and structure.

LanguageTool: Strong grammar checking with multilingual support and a less intrusive interface; AI features exist but are far less prominent and easier to ignore than Grammarly's current approach.

Should You Still Trust Grammarly?

Here's the honest answer: it depends what you're trusting it to do.

For catching typos, fixing comma errors, and identifying passive voice? Yes, absolutely. Those core features remain excellent, and the accuracy hasn't declined. Grammarly is still one of the best pure editing tools available. If you configure it to do only that, you can continue using it with confidence.

For preserving your authentic voice while offering writing suggestions? That trust has been damaged, and rightfully so. The current implementation of AI features prioritizes generic clarity over individual style. Until Grammarly develops better voice-matching capabilities — and there's no indication that's a priority — writers who care deeply about voice should approach generative features with skepticism.

For knowing where your writing ends and AI assistance begins? This is where the trust issue is most serious. The interface doesn't clearly distinguish between "this comma should move" and "here are three sentences I wrote for you." That lack of transparency is a problem, especially for professional writers who need to know exactly what they're putting their name on.

The path forward for concerned users is straightforward: audit your Grammarly settings today, disable features you don't want, and be intentional about what you're asking the tool to do. The version of Grammarly that earned your trust still exists — you just have to explicitly choose it now instead of getting it by default. And if you find yourself constantly fighting against suggestions that feel wrong, that's feedback worth listening to. A tool that makes you second-guess your own judgment isn't helping, no matter how sophisticated its AI is.

Watch for Grammarly's response over the next few months. If they introduce clearer controls, better voice-matching, and more obvious distinctions between editing and generating, that's a sign they're listening. If they double down on generative features and make them harder to disable, that's a sign the company's priorities have shifted permanently away from the writers who built their user base. Your next move should depend on which direction they choose.
