Beehiiv vs. Substack: The Feud That's Splitting the Creator Economy

June 22, 2026 7 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

Three years ago, launching a newsletter meant wrestling with clunky WordPress plugins and praying your email deliverability held up. Now, platforms like Beehiiv and Substack promise one-click publishing, built-in audiences, and revenue from day one. But beneath that convenience, a bitter rivalry has turned into something uglier: public feuds, creator poaching allegations, and a community split down the middle over which platform actually serves writers versus which one exploits them.

What started as friendly competition between two newsletter platforms has escalated into one of the most divisive debates in the creator economy. Beehiiv, the scrappy challenger founded by former Morning Brew executives, has been aggressively courting Substack's top earners with cash incentives and feature promises. Substack, meanwhile, has accused Beehiiv of predatory tactics and questioned whether their rival's growth metrics are even real. Creators are choosing sides, investors are watching nervously, and thousands of writers are wondering if they backed the wrong horse.

How a Feature War Became a Public Brawl

The tension started quietly in late 2024 when Beehiiv began offering five-figure signing bonuses to lure prominent Substack writers. Not just anyone—specifically creators with subscriber lists over 50,000 and proven monetization. The pitch was simple: bring your audience to Beehiiv, get cash upfront, and access better growth tools. Several high-profile creators accepted, including a tech newsletter that brought 200,000 subscribers and a political commentator with a six-figure annual income.

Substack's response came in March 2025 through a blog post from co-founder Hamish McKenzie that many interpreted as a direct shot. Without naming Beehiiv, he criticized platforms that "treat writers like interchangeable content units to be bought and sold" and warned creators about tools that prioritize growth metrics over reader relationships. The post sparked immediate backlash from Beehiiv's community, with CEO Tyler Denk firing back on Twitter that Substack was "terrified of real competition" and "hiding behind virtue signaling."

What followed was months of escalating public spats. Beehiiv released comparison charts showing their referral program generated 3x more subscribers than Substack's. Substack published reader surveys claiming Beehiiv newsletters had 40% lower open rates. Both platforms started hosting competing conferences. Creators began posting callout threads accusing the other side of astroturfing, fake testimonials, and cherry-picked data. By early 2026, the feud had its own Reddit thread with over 15,000 members tracking every accusation.

The breaking point came in May 2026 when leaked internal Slack messages from Beehiiv appeared to show sales staff discussing "target lists" of Substack's top 500 earners, complete with estimated annual revenue and suggested offer amounts. Beehiiv claimed the messages were taken out of context and part of normal competitive research. Substack called it proof of "coordinated creator poaching." The leak dominated creator economy Twitter for a week, with writers splitting into camps and questioning the ethics of both platforms.

What Creators Are Actually Fighting About

Beneath the drama, there are legitimate concerns on both sides that go beyond tribal loyalty. Substack creators worry that Beehiiv's aggressive growth tactics prioritize list size over reader quality. Several writers who switched reported that while their subscriber counts grew faster on Beehiiv, engagement rates dropped and paid conversion suffered. One food newsletter saw subscribers jump from 80,000 to 140,000 in six months after moving to Beehiiv, but paid subscribers actually declined from 2,400 to 1,900. The theory: Beehiiv's referral incentives and pop-up forms attract freebie seekers, not engaged readers.

There's also tension around platform philosophy. Substack has consistently positioned itself as creator-first, taking a flat 10% cut of subscription revenue and promising never to insert ads or algorithmically suppress content. Writers own their subscriber lists completely and can export everything with one click. That predictability matters to creators building long-term businesses. When you're earning $15,000 monthly from 2,000 paid subscribers, platform stability isn't abstract—it's your mortgage.

Beehiiv creators counter that Substack's purity is actually rigidity that limits growth. They point to features Substack refuses to build: advanced segmentation, A/B testing, custom landing pages, affiliate programs, and native ad networks for creators who want sponsorship revenue alongside subscriptions. Beehiiv offers all of this, plus automation tools that feel more like Make.com than a simple publishing platform. For creators treating newsletters as full businesses rather than digital journals, those features matter more than philosophical positioning.

The data privacy angle adds another layer. Both platforms claim to be privacy-respecting, but Beehiiv's analytics are significantly more detailed—tracking individual subscriber behavior, engagement patterns, and even estimated likelihood to upgrade to paid. That power is useful for optimization, but some creators are uncomfortable with the surveillance implications. One privacy-focused tech writer called it "turning readers into data points" and cited it as the reason she stayed on Substack despite Beehiiv offering her $25,000 to switch.

Then there's the money question: are Beehiiv's signing bonuses sustainable or a cash burn that ends badly? The platform raised $33 million in Series A funding in 2024, and critics argue they're subsidizing creator acquisition with investor money that will eventually run out. If Beehiiv needs to become profitable and can't keep paying creators to join, what happens to the people who switched expecting ongoing support? Beehiiv insists their revenue from ads, premium features, and transaction fees already covers costs, but they haven't released detailed financials to back that claim.

Beehiiv's Defense: Why They Think They're the Good Guys

Tyler Denk and the Beehiiv team argue they're being vilified for doing what any competitive business should do: offering creators better tools and fair compensation for switching. In a May 2026 blog post, Denk wrote that Substack's criticism is "monopolistic gatekeeping disguised as ethics" and that paying creators to migrate is no different from how every SaaS company offers switching incentives.

Beehiiv's position is that Substack's 10% cut is actually expensive for successful creators. A newsletter earning $200,000 annually pays Substack $20,000 for what Beehiiv argues is a basic service. Beehiiv's pricing starts free and scales to $99-$300 monthly for advanced features, regardless of revenue—potentially saving big earners tens of thousands. They frame signing bonuses as advance savings that creators would have realized anyway.

On the engagement rate criticism, Beehiiv published a counter-analysis claiming that when controlling for content quality and niche, there's no meaningful difference in open rates between platforms. They argue that creators who see drops are either comparing apples to oranges or experiencing natural audience maturation that would happen anywhere. They also point out that Substack's lack of analytics makes it harder for writers to actually know if their engagement is good—creating a false sense of quality through ignorance.

The team also emphasizes that Beehiiv was built by people who actually ran a successful newsletter (Morning Brew). They understand creator needs from experience, not theory, and that's why their feature set is more comprehensive. Substack, they argue, is built by tech idealists who prioritize philosophical purity over practical utility.

What This Means If You're Choosing a Platform Right Now

If you're starting a newsletter in mid-2026 or considering switching, this feud clarifies your decision more than it complicates it. The two platforms serve genuinely different creator profiles, and the controversy makes those differences impossible to ignore.

Choose Substack if you want simplicity, predictability, and you plan to monetize primarily through paid subscriptions. The 10% cut hurts less when you're earning your first $5,000 than it does at $200,000, and the platform's focus on reader relationships over growth hacking suits writers who prioritize quality audiences. Substack's ecosystem also has network effects—readers discover new writers through recommendations and the Substack app. If you write in categories like politics, culture, or long-form journalism where reader loyalty matters most, Substack's approach makes sense.

Choose Beehiiv if you're treating your newsletter like a media business and want sophisticated tools to match. The analytics, automation, and monetization options support creators who want to run A/B tests, segment audiences, and diversify revenue beyond subscriptions. The pricing structure heavily favors high earners, making it a clear winner if you're already monetizing successfully or plan to scale aggressively. Just be realistic about whether those advanced features will actually improve your newsletter or just distract you from writing.

The signing bonus angle is trickier. If you're already successful on Substack and Beehiiv offers you money to switch, treat it like any business decision: calculate what you'd save in platform fees over two years, compare features honestly, and don't let a one-time payment override long-term fit. Several creators who took bonuses later said they regretted switching because the feature benefits didn't match the migration hassle.

One underappreciated consideration: export flexibility. Both platforms let you take your subscriber list if you leave, but Beehiiv's infrastructure is more complex, meaning migration away from them could be harder than leaving Substack's simpler setup. If platform independence matters—if you might eventually want to move to ConvertKit, Ghost, or a custom solution—starting on the simpler platform gives you more flexibility.

The Pattern This Reveals About Creator Platforms

This feud isn't just about newsletters—it's a preview of how every creator economy platform will compete as the market matures. The same dynamics are already playing out between YouTube and TikTok, Spotify and Apple Podcasts, Patreon and Buy Me A Coffee. Established platforms lean on simplicity and ecosystem effects while upstarts compete on features and financial incentives.

What makes this case particularly instructive is how quickly "creator-first" rhetoric becomes weaponized. Both Beehiiv and Substack claim to serve creators better than the other, but their definitions of service are incompatible. Substack defines it as protection from algorithmic manipulation and revenue extraction. Beehiiv defines it as powerful tools and fair pricing. Both are valid, but they appeal to creators with different priorities and business models.

The controversy also highlights how little transparency exists in this space. Neither platform publishes detailed engagement benchmarks, churn rates, or average creator earnings. The leaked Slack messages were revealing precisely because they showed what competitive intelligence companies actually track versus what they share publicly. Creators are making business decisions with incomplete information, and platforms benefit from that opacity.

There's also a cautionary lesson about platform dependency. The creators most stressed by this feud are those who built entire businesses on one platform without diversification. Writers who also have podcasts, YouTube channels, or owned websites feel less anxious because newsletter platforms are just one distribution channel. The most platform-locked creators are essentially employees of Substack or Beehiiv without the benefits of employment—no healthcare, no equity, no employment protections. When the platforms fight, those creators feel the collateral damage most acutely.

Worth Considering: Alternatives to Both

If this entire drama makes both platforms feel unappealing, several alternatives exist with different trade-offs:

  • ConvertKit: Email marketing platform built specifically for creators with powerful automation but requires more setup than Substack or Beehiiv; pricing starts at $15/month for up to 300 subscribers.
  • Ghost: Open-source publishing platform you can self-host or use their managed service; gives you complete ownership and control but requires more technical comfort; starts at $11/month.
  • Kit (formerly ConvertKit): Strong automation features similar to Make.com but focused on email marketing; better for creators who want sophisticated workflows without the Beehiiv complexity drama; pricing scales with list size.

Should You Switch Platforms or Stay Put?

Here's what actually matters: if your current platform is working—you're growing, earning, and not hitting feature limitations—this feud is entertainment, not a crisis requiring action. Platform switching is expensive in time and subscriber confusion, even when export tools work perfectly. Don't let drama push you into a migration that doesn't serve your business.

If you're genuinely unhappy with your current platform's limitations, this is a good moment to evaluate alternatives because both Substack and Beehiiv are aggressively trying to retain and attract creators. You have negotiating leverage. Multiple creators have reported that when they told Substack they were considering Beehiiv, Substack offered beta access to features previously unavailable. Competition works.

The creators who should genuinely consider switching are those earning over $100,000 annually on Substack, where Beehiiv's flat-rate pricing could save substantial money. Run the math: if you're earning $150,000 yearly, you're paying Substack $15,000 in fees. Beehiiv would charge you $300 monthly ($3,600 annually) for comparable features, saving you $11,400 yearly. Over three years, that's $34,200—enough to justify the migration hassle if the features align with your needs.

Watch for how both platforms evolve their policies over the next year. The controversy has created pressure on both sides to prove their models are sustainable and creator-friendly. Substack will need to add features to compete with Beehiiv's capabilities, and Beehiiv will need to demonstrate that their growth metrics translate to real creator success, not just vanity numbers. The platform that adapts while maintaining its core strengths will likely win the long game.

Most importantly, don't let anyone—platforms, influencers, or fellow creators—convince you that choosing one side makes you morally superior. This is business software, not a political movement. Pick the tool that serves your specific newsletter goals, understand its trade-offs clearly, and stay alert to when those trade-offs stop working for you. That's not tribalism or disloyalty; it's being a professional.
