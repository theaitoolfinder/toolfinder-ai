Why Grammarly's AI Upgrade Is Making Writers Trust It Less, Not More

June 18, 2026 7 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

Grammarly's latest AI-powered writing assistant was supposed to make the tool smarter, more intuitive, and more helpful. Instead, it's doing something the company didn't anticipate: making experienced writers trust the platform less. After two decades of building credibility as the grammar checker that caught what you missed, Grammarly's aggressive push into AI-generated suggestions, tone rewriting, and contextual content generation has created a paradox. The more capable the tool becomes, the more writers question whether they're getting genuine help or algorithmic interference. This isn't about feature bugs or pricing complaints—it's about a fundamental shift in how the tool positions itself in the creative process, and why that shift feels uncomfortable to the people who've relied on it longest.

What Changed: From Safety Net to Co-Writer

For most of its existence, Grammarly occupied a clear role in the writing process. It was the final pass—the eagle-eyed editor who caught typos, flagged unclear phrasing, and reminded you that "their" and "there" aren't interchangeable. Writers knew exactly what they were getting: a proofreading tool with some style suggestions thrown in. The red underlines meant something was wrong. The blue ones meant something could be better. Simple.

In early 2026, Grammarly launched what it called its "most significant platform evolution since founding." The update integrated large language model capabilities directly into the core editing experience. Now, instead of just flagging a passive voice construction, Grammarly offers to rewrite entire paragraphs. Instead of suggesting you reconsider a word choice, it generates three alternative sentences with different tones. The AI can now draft email responses, expand bullet points into full paragraphs, and suggest structural changes to your arguments.

On paper, this sounds like pure upside. More capability, more intelligence, more help. But here's what actually happened: writers who opened Grammarly to check their work found themselves staring at suggestions that didn't just correct their writing—they replaced it. One freelance journalist described opening a draft article and seeing Grammarly suggest rewrites for nearly every sentence, not because they were grammatically wrong, but because the AI had "better" alternatives. Another content creator reported that Grammarly's new AI started suggesting she change her conversational tone to something more formal, despite her entire brand being built on casual, relatable writing.

The complaints started small—confused posts on Reddit, questions in writing communities—but they built into something larger. Writers weren't upset that Grammarly had gotten worse at catching errors. They were upset that it had stopped behaving like a tool they controlled and started behaving like a collaborator they hadn't asked for.

Why Writers Feel Like They're Losing Control

The concerns fall into three distinct categories, and they're more nuanced than simple technophobia. First, there's the trust erosion issue. When Grammarly only caught genuine errors, you could trust its judgment implicitly. A red underline meant you'd made a mistake. Now, when the tool suggests rewriting a perfectly grammatical sentence because its AI thinks it could be "more engaging," writers face a different question: is this correction or opinion? The line between objective error and stylistic preference has blurred, and that blurs the tool's authority.

Several professional writers I spoke with described a new hesitation when reviewing Grammarly's suggestions. They found themselves second-guessing both the tool and their own judgment. Did that sentence really need changing, or is the AI just generating alternatives because it can? This isn't paranoia—it's a rational response to a tool that no longer signals clear boundaries between correction and suggestion.

Second, there's the voice homogenization concern. Writers develop distinct styles—the way they structure sentences, their word choices, their rhythm. These aren't errors to be fixed; they're signatures. But AI language models are trained on massive datasets and tend toward a kind of statistical average of "good writing." When Grammarly suggests AI-generated alternatives, those suggestions often feel polished but generic. They're technically better by some metrics—clearer, more concise, more conventional—but they strip away the idiosyncrasies that make individual voices recognizable.

One novelist described testing the new features on a draft chapter and finding that Grammarly's AI suggestions made her prose "sound like every other AI-edited manuscript I've seen lately." The suggestions weren't wrong, but they were flattening. For writers whose value proposition is their unique perspective and voice, that's not a feature improvement—it's an existential threat.

Third, there's the uncertainty about what's being "helped" and what's being rewritten. This matters particularly for professional contexts. If you're a journalist and Grammarly's AI suggests a better way to phrase a quote's context, did you write that transition or did the AI? If you're an academic and the tool restructures your argument for clarity, how do you cite that? The old Grammarly made you a better writer by teaching you to catch your own mistakes. The new version can make your writing better without making you better at writing—and that distinction matters to people who write for a living.

Grammarly's Reasoning: Meeting Users Where AI Is Taking Them

To Grammarly's credit, the company hasn't ignored these concerns. In a detailed blog post published in March 2026, the product team explained their thinking. They pointed out that user research showed people increasingly wanted more than proofreading. They wanted help getting started, overcoming blank page paralysis, and adapting their tone for different contexts. The AI features weren't meant to replace writers—they were meant to handle the mechanical parts of writing so creators could focus on ideas and strategy.

The company also emphasized control. All AI-generated suggestions are clearly labeled as such, and users can adjust the aggressiveness of AI recommendations in settings. You can tell Grammarly to only flag clear errors and ignore stylistic AI suggestions entirely. The company's position is essentially: we're giving you more capability, but you're still in the driver's seat. If you don't want AI suggestions, turn them off.

There's also a competitive argument here. Tools like Wordtune, Copy.ai, and Jasper AI have been offering AI-powered writing assistance for years. ChatGPT and Claude can rewrite, restructure, and generate content in seconds. If Grammarly had stayed purely as a grammar checker, it would have risked irrelevance. The company was adapting to a market where writing assistance increasingly means AI collaboration, not just error correction.

What This Actually Means If You're a Daily Grammarly User

Here's the practical reality: if you're using Grammarly the way you always have—as a final check before hitting send—this change might barely affect you. The core grammar and spelling checks still work exactly as they did. The tool still catches typos, agreement errors, and unclear phrasing. If you ignore the AI suggestion cards and focus on the traditional underlines, you're essentially using the old Grammarly with a busier interface.

But if you've started clicking "accept" on AI suggestions without reading them carefully, you should stop. That's where the trust erosion happens. Treat AI-generated rewrites the way you'd treat a colleague's editing suggestions: read them critically, accept what genuinely improves your work, and reject what changes your voice or meaning. The problem isn't that the AI suggestions exist—it's when users start trusting them blindly because they're coming from a tool they've always trusted.

For professional writers, the advice is more specific: adjust your settings. Grammarly lets you dial down AI suggestions or disable them entirely for specific document types. If you're working on something where voice matters—creative work, personal essays, brand content—turn the AI features to minimal or off. Use the tool the way you used it in 2024. It still works that way; you just have to tell it that's what you want.

For people who actually want AI writing help, the new features are legitimately useful. If you're drafting routine emails, generating meeting recaps (where tools like Fireflies.ai might also help with transcription), or writing content where efficiency matters more than personal voice, Grammarly's AI suggestions can speed up your process. The key is matching the tool's mode to the task's requirements.

This Isn't Just About Grammarly—It's About Every AI Tool's Identity Crisis

What's happening with Grammarly is happening across the AI tools ecosystem. Tools that built their reputations doing one thing well are now facing pressure to become AI-everything platforms. Canva, which started as a simplified design tool, now has AI image generation and content creation. Make.com and Zapier, which automated workflows, now promote AI-powered automation builders. Even specialized tools like NeuronWriter, which focused on SEO content optimization, have layered in AI writing assistance.

The pattern is the same: companies see the capabilities that large language models offer and feel compelled to integrate them, even when it means their tools start doing things users didn't ask for. Sometimes this creates genuine value—ElevenLabs' AI voice cloning wouldn't exist without embracing bleeding-edge AI. But sometimes it just creates feature bloat and confusion about what a tool is actually for.

The broader question is whether AI tools can maintain user trust while dramatically expanding their capabilities. Grammarly's challenge is that it built trust by being conservative and reliable. It didn't try to be creative or clever—it just caught mistakes you missed. Now it's trying to be an AI writing partner, and that requires a different kind of trust that users haven't necessarily given it yet. The company is asking users to trust not just that it knows correct grammar, but that it knows what good writing looks like in general. That's a much bigger ask.

This mirrors what's happening with AI assistants broadly. People are comfortable with AI that augments specific tasks—transcription, scheduling, image editing. They're much more hesitant about AI that tries to augment creative or intellectual work where judgment and voice matter. Grammarly is caught in that transition, trying to be both the reliable proofreader and the creative collaborator, and discovering those roles might actually conflict.

Worth Considering: Alternatives That Still Know Their Lane

If Grammarly's evolution doesn't fit your needs anymore, you have options that maintain clearer boundaries:

  • ProWritingAid: Offers detailed writing reports and grammar checking with AI features that are optional and clearly separated from core editing.
  • Hemingway Editor: Focuses specifically on clarity and readability without trying to rewrite your work—it highlights issues but leaves the fixing to you.
  • LanguageTool: A more traditional grammar checker that supports multiple languages and hasn't jumped into generative AI territory yet.

For users who actually want AI writing collaboration, tools like Claude, Wordtune, or even using ChatGPT directly might be more honest approaches—they don't pretend to be grammar checkers with AI features bolted on; they're AI writing assistants from the ground up, and users engage with them knowing that's what they are.

The Bottom Line: Grammarly Still Works, But You Need Boundaries Now

Is Grammarly still worth using in mid-2026? For most people, yes—but with a different mental model than before. It's no longer the tool you trust implicitly and accept every suggestion from. It's a tool with two distinct modes: reliable grammar checking and experimental AI assistance. You need to decide which mode you want for each project and configure the tool accordingly.

The trust issue is real, but it's not insurmountable. Grammarly hasn't stopped being good at catching errors—it's just started offering things beyond error correction, and those additional offers can feel intrusive if you weren't asking for them. The solution isn't necessarily to abandon the tool; it's to reassert control over how you use it. Disable AI features for work where voice matters. Enable them for routine tasks where efficiency is the priority. Treat AI suggestions skeptically and only accept them when they genuinely improve your work by your standards, not the algorithm's.

What would rebuild trust? Clearer defaults that match how people actually use the tool. A "Classic Mode" that disables all AI features and returns to the 2024 experience. Better visual distinction between "this is wrong" and "this could be different." More transparency about when the AI is suggesting changes based on grammar rules versus statistical patterns in its training data. These aren't impossible asks—they're design choices that prioritize user control over feature showcasing.

The writers who are trusting Grammarly less aren't rejecting AI or resisting progress. They're responding to a tool that changed its relationship with them without asking first. Grammarly positioned itself for 20 years as the assistant that helped you be a better writer. Now it's positioning itself as an AI that can write for you. Those are fundamentally different value propositions, and the company is learning that you can't make that shift without some users feeling like the tool they trusted has become something else entirely.
