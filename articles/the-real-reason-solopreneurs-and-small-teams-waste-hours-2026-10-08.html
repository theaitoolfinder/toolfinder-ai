The Real Reason Solopreneurs and Small Teams Waste hours untangling three different apps (And the Taskade AI Fix for It)

October 08, 2026 7 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

You opened your to-do list app this morning to check what's due today. Then you switched to your notes app to find the brief from last week's client call. Then you jumped into your project management tool to see who was supposed to handle the design file. Fifteen minutes and six browser tabs later, you finally found what you needed—but now you're behind on everything else, and you haven't actually started working yet.

This is the daily tax that solopreneurs and small teams pay when their workflow is split across Todoist for tasks, Notion for notes, and Trello for project tracking. Each app works fine on its own, but none of them talk to each other. So you spend hours every week playing information archaeologist: digging through three different apps to find the context you need, manually copying updates between them, and trying to remember which app holds which piece of the puzzle.

The cost is real and measurable. A typical solopreneur loses 6–8 hours per week just to this context-switching overhead—time that could be spent on billable work, product development, or finally getting ahead instead of constantly catching up. For a two-person team, that number doubles. At a conservative freelance rate of $75/hour, you're burning $450–$600 every single week on digital housekeeping. Over a year, that's $23,400–$31,200 of lost productivity.

Beyond the time cost, there's the mental load. Every project requires you to hold a mental map of where everything lives: client feedback is in Slack, the actual deliverable checklist is in Asana, meeting notes are in Google Docs, and your personal task list is in yet another app. You're not just managing your work—you're managing the system that manages your work. And when a client asks a simple question like "what's the status on that logo revision?", you need to consult three different sources before you can answer with confidence.

The fragmentation also creates gaps where critical information falls through. You write detailed notes during a strategy call but forget to create the corresponding tasks in your to-do app. A week later, you've missed the deadline because the action items never made it out of your notes document. Or you update your project board but forget to document the reasoning in your notes, so when you revisit the project three months later, you're reverse-engineering your own decisions.

Why the Obvious Fixes Don't Work

Most people try solving this with Zapier or Make.com automations, connecting their three apps with automated workflows. This works in theory, but in practice you're now maintaining a fourth system—the automation layer itself. Every time one of your apps updates its API or changes a field name, your automations break. You spend an hour troubleshooting why tasks aren't syncing properly, only to discover that Notion changed how it handles database properties last week.

Others try consolidating everything into Notion alone. It's flexible enough to handle tasks, notes, and projects in one place. But Notion wasn't built as a task manager first—it's a documentation tool that can sort of do tasks if you build the right structure. You end up with a complex database setup that requires twenty minutes of YouTube tutorials to understand, and simple questions like "what am I working on right now?" require filtering three linked databases. The cognitive overhead of maintaining your Notion system becomes its own part-time job.

The "just pick one app" advice falls apart when you realize that single-purpose tools are purpose-built. Todoist is genuinely better at task management than any general-purpose tool. Notion is genuinely better at long-form documentation. Trello is genuinely better at visual project tracking. That's why you picked them in the first place. Forcing everything into one specialized tool means compromising on the features that made you choose it originally.

What Changes Once This Is Solved

Imagine starting your workday by opening a single workspace where your tasks, project context, meeting notes, and team updates all live in the same view—not as separate sections you toggle between, but as connected information that updates together automatically. You check your agenda, and the relevant notes and action items are already there, attached to each meeting. You mark a task complete, and the project board updates instantly without you touching it.

When a client emails asking about project status, you don't hunt through three apps. You open one project page and everything is there: what's done, what's in progress, what's blocked, and the notes explaining why decisions were made. You answer in thirty seconds instead of ten minutes.

Those 6–8 hours per week you were spending on context-switching and manual updates? They disappear. You're not managing your tools anymore—you're just working. The mental load drops because there's only one place to look, one system to maintain, one source of truth. And when you need to hand off a project to a contractor or bring on a team member, you share one link instead of explaining a three-app workflow they need to learn.

This isn't about marginal improvement—it's about removing an entire category of work from your week. The work that doesn't create value, doesn't move projects forward, and exists only because your tools don't talk to each other.

Exactly How to Use It to Solve This

Step 1: Create your first unified project workspace. Sign up for Taskade (start with the free plan to test it) and create a new project. Instead of thinking "is this a document or a task list?", just dump everything related to one active project into a single Taskade page. Client brief at the top, meeting notes in the middle, action items at the bottom. Use Taskade's outline structure—bullet points nest infinitely, so your project naturally organizes itself as you add information.

Step 2: Convert your scattered notes and tasks into connected items. Take any bullet point and press Cmd+Enter (Mac) or Ctrl+Enter (Windows) to convert it into a task with a checkbox. It stays exactly where it is in your notes, but now it's also trackable. Add a due date by typing "@" followed by the date, or assign it to yourself with "/assign". The task is now in your master task list (accessible from the sidebar) but still connected to the project context where it originated.

Here's a real example: You're planning a product launch. Your Taskade project starts with notes from your planning meeting: "Target launch date is November 15. Need landing page, email sequence, and social content. Main message: solving the onboarding problem for B2B SaaS teams." Below that, you add action items as nested bullets. Select "Draft landing page copy" and hit Cmd+Enter—it's now a task. Add "@Nov 1" and it has a deadline. The task appears in your Today view when November 1 arrives, but when you open it, you can see the full launch context above it—the target date, the messaging, everything. You're not hunting through a separate notes app to remember what this task is actually about.

Step 3: Use AI to fill gaps and generate next steps. This is where the unified structure pays off. Highlight your meeting notes and click the AI button, then prompt it: "Based on these launch notes, create a complete task list with realistic deadlines working backward from November 15." Taskade AI reads the context, understands the dependencies (you need copy before design, design before development), and generates a structured task breakdown—each item already formatted as a checkable task with suggested dates. You review, adjust dates, and you're done. What would take 30 minutes of manual planning took 90 seconds.

You can also use AI for project-specific questions. With your launch project open, ask "What still needs to be done before we can start the email sequence?" Taskade AI scans your tasks, notes, and completed items, then tells you exactly what's blocking that next step. It's reading the same unified workspace you are, so the answers are grounded in your actual project state, not generic advice.

Step 4: Switch views without switching apps. At the top of your project, toggle between List view (outline/notes format), Board view (kanban-style columns), and Calendar view (timeline). Your content doesn't change—just how you're looking at it. When you're planning, use List view to think through details. When you're executing, switch to Board view to see what's in progress versus done. When a client asks about timeline, switch to Calendar view and take a screenshot. It's the same project data, just three different lenses on demand.

Step 5: Repeat for every project. Create a new Taskade project for each major initiative or client. Your sidebar becomes a list of active projects, each one containing all tasks, notes, and context in one place. When you need to find something, you open the relevant project—not three different apps. When you need to see all your tasks across all projects, Taskade's "My Tasks" view aggregates everything due today or this week, with links back to the source project for context.

"But What If…" — Answering the Real Objections

Objection 1: "I've already built my entire system in Notion/Todoist/etc. Migration sounds like a nightmare." Start small—don't migrate everything at once. Pick your next new project and build it entirely in Taskade. Run both systems in parallel for two weeks. You'll quickly feel the difference between opening one Taskade project versus juggling three apps for the new work. Once that's clear, migrate one existing project per week. Taskade imports from Notion, Trello, Asana, and Todoist via CSV or direct copy-paste, so the actual technical migration takes minutes per project. The real barrier is psychological, not technical.

Objection 2: "Will I really use the AI features, or am I paying for something I'll ignore?" The AI in Taskade isn't a separate feature you have to remember to use—it's embedded in the places you're already working. You're writing notes and the AI can expand them into full outlines. You're staring at a task list and the AI can suggest what's missing. You're stuck on how to phrase something and the AI can rewrite it. It's more like autocomplete on steroids than a separate tool you have to invoke. Even if you only use it a few times per week to generate task breakdowns or summarize notes, you're still saving hours. And the free plan gives you 1,000 AI credits monthly to test this before paying.

Objection 3: "What if my workflow is too specific or my industry is too niche for a general tool?" Taskade's flexibility comes from its structure, not from pre-built templates. It's an outliner with tasks, not a rigid project management system. If you can represent your work as nested bullets (and almost every workflow can), Taskade handles it. Freelance writers use it for article research and drafts. Consultants use it for client projects and meeting notes. Event planners use it for vendor tracking and timeline management. The structure adapts to your needs because it's not forcing a specific methodology—it's just organized information with the ability to mark items as actionable.
