Zapier vs Make.com: Pricing, Features, and Which One Wins for Most Users

June 21, 2026 8 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

Six months ago, setting up a workflow to sync customer data from Typeform to Google Sheets, then trigger a personalized email via Gmail, took me four hours of wrestling with API documentation. Now? It takes 18 minutes with the right automation tool. But here's the catch: I wasted three days and $87 testing the wrong tool first.

If you're staring at Zapier and Make.com (formerly Integromat) trying to figure out which one deserves your money and time, you're facing a decision that will ripple through every automated workflow you build for the next year. Pick wrong, and you'll either overpay for simplicity you've outgrown, or drown in complexity you don't need yet. Pick right, and you'll automate processes that currently eat 10+ hours of your week.

This isn't about which tool has more integrations on paper. It's about which one actually works for your brain, your budget, and the specific automations keeping you up at night. I've built workflows in both platforms, hit their limits, cursed at their quirks, and figured out exactly when each one wins.

  • Best for beginners who value speed over cost: Zapier — you'll have your first automation running in under 10 minutes, even if you've never touched an API.
  • Best for users who build complex, multi-step workflows: Make.com — visual builder makes intricate logic actually comprehensible, and you get way more operations per dollar.
  • Best if you're budget-tight but willing to learn: Make.com — free tier gives you 1,000 operations monthly versus Zapier's 100 tasks, and paid plans start cheaper.

Zapier: The Tool That Spoils You With Simplicity (Until the Bill Arrives)

Zapier wins on one metric that matters enormously when you're starting: you can build a working automation without reading documentation. The interface is linear—trigger happens, action follows, maybe add a filter, done. I built my first Zap (Zapier's term for a workflow) connecting Fireflies.ai meeting transcripts to a Notion database in about 12 minutes, including the time I spent hunting for my Notion API key.

The strength here is intentional limitation. Zapier doesn't show you 47 configuration options when 3 will do. When you're connecting Gmail to Google Sheets, it asks: "Which spreadsheet? Which sheet? What data goes in which columns?" You answer, you test, it works. The 5,000+ pre-built app integrations mean you're rarely hunting for a webhook URL or JSON formatter—someone already mapped the fields you need.

But here's where Zapier starts to hurt: pricing scales with task volume in a way that punishes success. The free tier gives you 100 tasks per month—sounds fine until you realize a single form submission that updates a sheet, sends an email, and logs to a CRM counts as three tasks. I hit my limit in 11 days during my first month.

Paid plans start at $19.99/month for 750 tasks, but you'll realistically need the $49/month Professional plan (2,000 tasks) once you're running multiple workflows. That same $49 gets you 10,000 operations on Make.com. If you're running an operation-heavy workflow—say, processing 50 OpusClip video exports daily with metadata updates—Zapier's math stops making sense fast.

The multi-step Zaps feature (anything beyond a single trigger and action) is paywalled behind the $19.99 tier. Folders to organize workflows? Also paid. Custom logic and error handling? You're looking at higher tiers. Zapier bets you'll pay for convenience, and for many users working inside straightforward workflows with moderate volume, that bet is correct.

Ideal Zapier user: You're a solopreneur or small team member who needs 3-8 reliable automations running with minimal maintenance. You'd rather pay $30-50/month than spend two hours learning a visual flow builder. Your workflows are mostly linear—one thing triggers, a few things happen, end of story. You value "set it and forget it" over customization.

Make.com: The Visual Builder That Rewards Complexity (With a Learning Curve Tax)

Make.com feels like moving from a checklist to a mind map. Instead of a vertical list of steps, you get a canvas where modules connect with lines. My first reaction was intimidation—there are routers, iterators, aggregators, filters sitting in the toolbar like tools I'd need a manual to understand. My second reaction, about 90 minutes later after building a workflow that pulled Beehiiv subscriber data, cross-referenced it with purchase history, and sent segmented emails based on three conditions, was: "Oh, I'm never building this in Zapier."

The visual interface makes conditional logic visible in a way linear builders can't match. When you need an automation that does X if condition A is true, Y if condition B is true, and Z if both are true simultaneously, Make.com lets you see those branches. I built a client onboarding workflow with five different paths depending on service tier, timezone, and whether they'd completed a typeform—trying to map that mentally in Zapier would've required documentation I'd never maintain.

Make.com's pricing structure rewards volume users dramatically. The free tier includes 1,000 operations monthly—10x what Zapier offers. The $9/month Core plan bumps you to 10,000 operations. Even the $16/month Pro plan (40,000 operations) costs less than Zapier's entry tier while offering vastly more capacity. If you're running automations that process hundreds of items—think daily social media scheduling with Predis.ai or batch transcription jobs—Make.com's math is unbeatable.

But that visual builder has a learning curve shaped like a wall. Understanding the difference between an iterator (processes array items one by one) and an aggregator (combines data from multiple modules) took me a genuine hour of trial, error, and Googling. Routers make sense conceptually but configuring them the first time feels like setting up mail filters in 1998. I spent 40 minutes on a workflow that would've taken 12 in Zapier, purely because I was learning Make.com's vocabulary.

The app integration library is smaller—around 1,500 apps versus Zapier's 5,000+. I've hit this limitation exactly twice: once looking for a niche CRM integration, once trying to connect an obscure scheduling tool. Both times I used Make.com's HTTP module to build a custom webhook connection, which worked but added 30 minutes to setup.

Ideal Make.com user: You're running or planning to run complex, high-volume workflows. You're comfortable spending a weekend learning a new interface if it saves you $40/month and unlocks capabilities you can't get elsewhere. You need conditional logic, data transformation, or workflows that branch based on multiple variables. You're processing hundreds to thousands of operations monthly.

Real-World Scenarios: Which Tool Wins and Why

Scenario 1: Content Creator Automating Social Distribution
You publish a YouTube video weekly. You want it to auto-post to Twitter, LinkedIn, and Facebook with platform-specific formatting, plus log the URL in a Google Sheet and notify your Slack channel.

Winner: Zapier. This is a straightforward linear workflow—one trigger (new YouTube video), five actions (three social posts, one sheet update, one Slack message). Total tasks per video: 6. Monthly volume at one video/week: 24 tasks. Zapier's $19.99 plan handles this comfortably. The simplicity means you set it up once and never think about it again. Make.com would work but offers no advantage here—you're paying for complexity you don't need.

Scenario 2: Agency Managing Client Onboarding
New clients fill a Typeform. Based on their service tier (basic/pro/enterprise) and timezone, you need to: assign them to the right project manager, create a customized Notion workspace, schedule a kickoff call at an appropriate time, send a service-specific welcome sequence via email, and create tasks in Asana with different templates per tier.

Winner: Make.com, decisively. You're looking at conditional branching (if tier = enterprise, do X, Y, Z; if tier = basic, do A, B, C), timezone logic, and data transformation. Make.com's routers let you visualize this—one path per service tier. The visual builder means when you need to update the enterprise path six months later, you can see exactly what happens without decoding a 23-step linear list. Volume is moderate (maybe 20 clients/month = 100+ operations) but complexity is high. Make.com's $9/month plan handles this; Zapier's equivalent would require the $49 tier and still feel clunky.

Scenario 3: E-commerce Store Syncing Data Daily
Every morning at 6 AM, you need to pull yesterday's Shopify orders, check inventory levels, update a Google Sheet dashboard, flag low-stock items in Slack, and generate a daily summary email. Average daily orders: 50.

Winner: Make.com by the numbers. You're processing 50 orders × 4-5 operations each = 200-250 operations daily, or 6,000-7,500 monthly. On Zapier, you'd need the $49/month plan minimum, possibly the $69 plan. Make.com's $9/month Core plan (10,000 operations) covers this with room to grow. The workflow itself isn't wildly complex—more volume-heavy than logic-heavy—but Make.com's pricing makes this a no-brainer.

The Verdict: Make.com Wins for Most Users in 2026

For the majority of users reading this—freelancers, solopreneurs, small teams building multiple automations—Make.com is the better investment. The initial learning curve is real but surmountable in a weekend. Once you're past it, you get 10-40x more operations per dollar and capabilities that Zapier simply can't match at any price point.

The tipping point is workflow complexity and volume. If you're running more than three automations, or any single automation processes more than 500 items monthly, Make.com's pricing advantage becomes impossible to ignore. The visual interface that feels overwhelming on day one becomes a massive asset by week three when you're maintaining and modifying workflows.

Zapier wins in exactly two scenarios: (1) You need a single, simple automation up and running this afternoon and have zero interest in learning a new interface. (2) You require an integration with a niche app that Make.com doesn't support and you can't/won't use webhooks. For everyone else—especially anyone planning to build 5+ workflows or process significant volume—the $40-60 you'll save monthly with Make.com pays for the weekend you spend learning it within six weeks.

If I were starting from zero today, I'd spend Saturday morning with Make.com's tutorials, build three practice workflows Sunday afternoon, and have my production automations running by Monday. The capability ceiling is higher, the pricing is friendlier, and the visual logic means I can hand off workflows to team members without writing documentation novels.

One tactical note: if you're currently on Zapier and considering switching, Make.com offers Zapier import for many common workflows. I tested it with four of my Zaps—two imported perfectly, one needed minor adjustments, one I rebuilt from scratch. Budget 30-60 minutes per workflow for migration.

Questions People Actually Ask Before Choosing

Can I use both Zapier and Make.com simultaneously?
Yes, and some users do—Zapier for simple, critical workflows where reliability matters more than cost, Make.com for complex or high-volume automations. I ran both for two months during transition. The mental overhead of remembering which automation lives where is real, though. Most users are better picking one and committing.

Which platform has better uptime and reliability?
Zapier reports 99.9%+ uptime and has a longer track record. Make.com's reliability has improved significantly—I've experienced zero downtime in eight months of daily use—but Zapier edges ahead on historical stability. For mission-critical workflows (payment processing, critical notifications), Zapier's maturity provides marginal extra confidence. For everything else, Make.com's reliability is production-ready.

What if I need an integration neither platform offers natively?
Both support webhooks and API calls. Make.com's HTTP module is more flexible and easier to configure than Zapier's equivalent (which requires paid tiers). If you're comfortable reading API documentation, Make.com actually handles custom integrations better. If you want pre-built, no-code connections only, Zapier's larger library wins. Third option: tools like Fireflies.ai, NeuronWriter, and ElevenLabs increasingly offer native webhook support, making platform choice less critical.
