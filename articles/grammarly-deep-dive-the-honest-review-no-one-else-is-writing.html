Grammarly Deep Dive: The Honest Review No One Else Is Writing

July 04, 2026 8 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

A $127 invoice. That's what a content marketing agency lost last month when a client rejected a blog post because of a single sentence fragment buried in paragraph nine. The writer had run it through Grammarly Premium. The client's in-house editor caught what Grammarly missed. The agency ate the revision costs, and the writer started questioning whether their $12/month subscription was actually doing its job.

I've been using Grammarly since 2019—back when it was just a glorified spell-checker with ambitions. Seven years later, it's morphed into something much bigger, much more opinionated, and frankly, much harder to evaluate. Everyone talks about Grammarly. Almost nobody tells you what it actually gets wrong, when it's actively unhelpful, or whether the AI features justify the price jump from $12 to $30/month that happened in early 2025.

This is the review I wish I'd read before renewing my subscription three times. No marketing spin. Just what Grammarly does well, what it screws up, and whether it's worth your money in 2026 when tools like NeuronWriter and ProWritingAid are breathing down its neck.

What Grammarly Actually Does (And Who's Behind It)

Grammarly is a writing assistant that checks your text for grammar errors, style issues, tone consistency, and—since late 2023—generates AI-powered rewrites and suggestions. It started in 2009 as a plagiarism checker for universities, pivoted to consumer grammar correction around 2015, and has spent the last decade becoming the default writing tool for anyone who writes more than emails.

The company is based in San Francisco and Kyiv, employs around 700 people, and has raised over $400 million in funding. That context matters because Grammarly isn't some scrappy startup anymore—it's a mature product with enterprise contracts, compliance certifications, and all the bureaucracy that comes with serving Fortune 500 companies. That's good for security. It's less good for innovation speed.

Here's how the pricing breaks down as of July 2026: Free gets you basic grammar and spelling checks. Premium costs $30/month (or $144/year if you pay upfront) and adds advanced grammar, style suggestions, plagiarism detection, and tone adjustments. Business is $25/user/month with team analytics and brand style guides. They killed the old $12/month Premium tier in February 2025 when they bundled in the AI features—a move that pissed off a lot of longtime users who just wanted grammar checks, not GPT-powered rewrites.

The tool lives everywhere: browser extension for Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge; desktop apps for Mac and Windows; mobile keyboards for iOS and Android; and direct integrations with Google Docs, Microsoft Word, Slack, and Gmail. That ubiquity is both Grammarly's biggest strength and its biggest liability—when it works, it's invisible. When it breaks, it breaks everywhere at once.

The One Thing Grammarly Does Better Than Anyone Else

Context-aware tone detection. Not the AI rewrite features—those are fine but not special. Not the grammar checking—ProWritingAid catches more advanced issues. The killer feature is Grammarly's ability to tell you how your writing will land emotionally before you hit send.

Here's how it works: Grammarly analyzes sentence structure, word choice, punctuation, and phrasing to classify your tone across nine dimensions—formal, informal, optimistic, worried, friendly, direct, confident, cautious, and respectful. It shows you a real-time tone score as you type. When you're drafting an email to a client who's three weeks late on feedback, Grammarly will flag that your message reads as "demanding and frustrated" when you intended "firm but professional."

Before Grammarly's tone detector: "Just following up again on the feedback I requested two weeks ago. Really need this to move forward. Let me know when you can get to it." After adjusting based on tone suggestions: "Checking in on the feedback from our last meeting—no rush, but it would help me keep us on schedule if I could get your thoughts by Friday. Thanks for making time."

Same information. Completely different emotional landing. The first version tested as "direct and worried" with a formality mismatch. The second came back "friendly and confident" with appropriate professional framing. I've used this feature to save at least four client relationships and probably a dozen awkward Slack exchanges. No other tool reads tone this accurately—Wordtune tries but tends to make everything sound like a LinkedIn influencer wrote it.

Full Feature Breakdown: What Works, What Doesn't

Grammar and Spelling Corrections: This is table stakes, and Grammarly handles it well. It catches subject-verb disagreement, misplaced modifiers, comma splices, and the usual suspects. It misses about 15% of truly advanced grammatical errors—things like subtle parallelism breaks or complex conditional tense issues. For 90% of users, that's fine. If you're writing academic papers or legal documents, you need a human editor on top of this.

Clarity Suggestions: Grammarly flags wordy sentences, passive voice, and unclear antecedents. This feature is aggressive—sometimes too aggressive. It will tell you to cut "in order to" down to "to" even when the rhythm of the sentence needs those extra syllables. You learn to ignore about 30% of clarity suggestions once you understand your own writing style. Still useful for catching genuine bloat.

Plagiarism Detection (Premium only): Checks your text against 16 billion web pages and academic databases. It works, but it's slow—scanning a 2,000-word article takes 45-60 seconds. The results are thorough, showing exact matching phrases and similarity percentages. If you're a student or blogger worried about accidental duplication, it's solid. If you're a novelist, it's overkill and will flag common phrases as "matches" constantly.

AI Rewrites and Generative Features: This is what pushed the price from $12 to $30/month. You can highlight any sentence and ask Grammarly to make it more formal, more concise, more engaging, or completely rewrite it. The quality is... fine. It uses what feels like GPT-4 under the hood, but with heavy guardrails. Rewrites tend to be safe and bland. I use this feature maybe twice a week, and I write for a living. If you're not a professional writer, you might use it more. If you are, you'll find it dumbs down your voice.

Vocabulary Enhancement: Suggests stronger word choices and flags overused terms. This feature has gotten noticeably better since they integrated LLM-based semantic understanding in 2024. It used to suggest "utilize" for "use" like a bad intern trying to sound smart. Now it actually suggests contextually appropriate alternatives. Still occasionally misses register—it once suggested I replace "kids" with "juveniles" in a parenting blog post.

Browser Extension Performance: Works flawlessly in Gmail, Google Docs, and most standard text fields. Gets confused in Notion, breaks occasionally in WordPress (especially with page builders), and has a weird lag in Slack where suggestions appear 2-3 seconds after you stop typing. The extension also tanks battery life on MacBooks—I've measured a 15-20% reduction in battery duration when Grammarly is active across multiple tabs.

Brand Style Guide (Business tier only): Lets teams set custom rules for terminology, spelling preferences, and style choices. If your company spells "email" as "e-mail" or always capitalizes "Internet," you can enforce that. This is genuinely useful for content teams. It's also $300/year per person, which is absurd for what amounts to a fancy find-and-replace dictionary.

Real-World Test Results: Three Scenarios, Unfiltered Outcomes

Test 1: Editing a client blog post (1,850 words, B2B SaaS topic) I ran a draft article about API documentation through Grammarly Premium. Input: 1,850 words written in about 90 minutes, intentionally left rough. Grammarly flagged 43 issues: 7 grammar errors (all legitimate), 18 clarity suggestions (I accepted 11), 12 tone inconsistencies (accepted 8), and 6 engagement suggestions (ignored all—they wanted me to add rhetorical questions, which doesn't fit the client's style). Total editing time with Grammarly: 22 minutes. Without Grammarly, based on previous experience: 35-40 minutes. Time saved: About 15 minutes, but more importantly, I caught two errors that would've been embarrassing—a "their" that should've been "there" and a sentence fragment I completely missed on manual review.

Test 2: Writing a sensitive email to a delayed contractor This is where tone detection earned its keep. I drafted a message to a designer who'd missed two deadlines. First draft came back as "direct and disappointed" with a formality score of 68/100. Grammarly suggested four specific rephrasings. I took three. The revised email tested as "friendly and confident" with formality at 73/100. The contractor responded within an hour (unusual for him) and apologized without getting defensive. Could I have written that email well without Grammarly? Probably. Did Grammarly make it faster and more calibrated? Absolutely. Value here isn't replacement—it's calibration speed.

Test 3: Comparing plagiarism detection against a Beehiiv newsletter draft I write a weekly AI tools newsletter on Beehiiv. I pulled a standard industry stat about LLM adoption rates and phrased it in my own words. Grammarly's plagiarism checker flagged it as 12% similar to three other articles. Checked manually—those articles cited the same study using similar phrasing. Grammarly correctly showed me the overlap but couldn't distinguish between "this is plagiarism" and "this is just how people cite this specific study." A human editor would know the difference instantly. Grammarly doesn't. The feature is useful for catching accidental copy-paste errors, less useful for nuanced similarity judgments.

Who Should Actually Pay For This

Freelance writers producing 15+ pieces per month: If you're billing $0.10/word or higher and writing 30,000+ words monthly, the $144/year Premium plan pays for itself in saved editing time. You'll catch errors before clients do, and the tone detector will help you match different brand voices without as much manual recalibration. Just don't rely on it to replace a final human proofread on high-stakes work.

Non-native English speakers writing professional emails daily: This is Grammarly's sweet spot. The grammar correction is reliable enough to catch errors native speakers wouldn't make, and the formality/tone guidance helps navigate cultural communication differences. If English is your second or third language and you're emailing in it daily, Premium is worth every penny. You're essentially buying confidence and speed.

Content teams enforcing brand consistency (3+ writers): The Business tier makes sense only if you have at least three people writing under the same brand and you're currently spending 4+ hours/month manually enforcing style consistency. Below that threshold, just use a shared Google Doc with your style rules and save the $900/year for three seats.

Students writing research papers: The plagiarism checker alone justifies Premium during heavy writing semesters. Run every paper through it before submission. The grammar checking is good enough for academic writing, though you'll need to ignore some suggestions that don't understand discipline-specific conventions. Buy it for one semester, cancel it during breaks.

The Stuff Grammarly Gets Wrong (And Won't Tell You About)

Grammarly is bad at voice. If you have a distinctive writing style—especially if it's conversational, fragmented, or rhythmic—Grammarly will constantly fight you. It wants everything to sound like a corporate blog post written by someone who learned English from textbooks. I write with sentence fragments for emphasis. Grammarly hates that. It flags them every single time, even when they're intentional. You can dismiss suggestions, but you can't teach it your style. The "personal dictionary" only covers spelling, not syntax.

The AI rewrites are aggressively boring. I ran 20 sentences through the "make it more engaging" feature. Nineteen came back blander than the original. The one that improved was a genuinely clunky sentence that needed basic restructuring. If you're already a competent writer, this feature will make your work worse. If you're a weak writer, it might help, but you'd learn more from tools like Hemingway Editor that explain why something doesn't work.

Grammarly burns through system resources like it's rendering 4K video. On my M2 MacBook Air, the browser extension uses 180-250MB of RAM per active tab where I'm typing. That's absurd for a text editor. For comparison, the entire Hemingway Editor web app uses about 35MB. If you're on an older laptop or running a lot of tabs, you'll notice the slowdown.

It doesn't understand context across documents. Grammarly treats every piece of text as an island. If you're writing a series of blog posts on the same topic, it won't remember terminology you've established or stylistic choices you've made. Tools like NeuronWriter are starting to offer project-level context—Grammarly still doesn't.

Pricing Breakdown: What Each Tier Actually Gets You

Free tier: Basic grammar and spelling only. Useful if you just need to catch typos in emails. Not useful for anything professional. The suggestions are often teaser-locked—it'll tell you there's an issue but not how to fix it unless you upgrade. That's intentional friction, and it's annoying.

Premium ($30/month or $144/year): This is the tier most individuals should evaluate. You get full grammar checking, clarity and tone suggestions, plagiarism detection, and AI rewrites. The yearly plan saves you $216 compared to monthly. Worth it if you write professionally or frequently in high-stakes contexts. Not worth it if you're just drafting casual emails or personal blog posts—the free tier plus occasional manual editing would suffice.

Business ($25/user/month, minimum 3 seats): Only makes sense for teams actively managing brand voice across multiple writers. The analytics dashboard shows team-wide writing stats (average readability, common errors, etc.), which is mildly interesting but rarely actionable. The style guide feature is the real value, and it's only worth $25/month if you're currently paying someone to manually enforce style consistency.

Here's the math that matters: Premium breaks even if it saves you 5 hours per year at a $29/hour effective rate. For most professionals, that's 20-25 minutes per month. If you write anything public-facing or client-facing at least weekly, you'll clear that bar. If you write once or twice a month, stick with the free tier and budget for a human editor on important pieces.

How Grammarly Stacks Up Against the Competition

ProWritingAid catches more advanced grammar issues and offers deeper style reports, but it's clunkier to use and the browser extension is noticeably slower. Choose ProWritingAid if you're editing long-form content (novels, reports, thesis papers) and you have time to review detailed analysis. Choose Grammarly if you need real-time feedback across emails, docs, and web forms.

Wordtune is better at AI rewrites but worse at grammar checking. Its rephrasing suggestions are more creative and varied than Grammarly's. But it misses basic grammar errors that Grammarly catches instantly. If you're already a clean writer and want help with phrasing, try Wordtune. If you need comprehensive error-catching, Grammarly wins.

Hemingway Editor is better for learning to write clearly. It explains why something is hard to read and forces you to understand the fix. Grammarly just tells you what to change. For improving as a writer, Hemingway is superior. For speed and convenience, Grammarly dominates. I use both—Hemingway for drafts where I'm learning, Grammarly for production writing where I need speed.
