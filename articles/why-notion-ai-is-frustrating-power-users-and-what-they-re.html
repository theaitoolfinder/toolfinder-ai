Why Notion AI Is Frustrating Power Users — and What They're Switching To

June 19, 2026 7 min read By Papabenj · My AI Tools Finder

You're managing a 50-page product roadmap in Notion, you've templated your entire project management system, and you just asked Notion AI to summarize your sprint retrospective notes. It gives you three generic bullet points that miss the actual tension your team discussed, and you realize you're paying $10/month for what feels like a basic autocomplete feature bolted onto a tool you already loved. If this sounds familiar, you're part of a growing segment of power users who feel Notion AI promised intelligence but delivered parlor tricks.

The frustration isn't just about mediocre summaries. It's about watching a tool you've invested hundreds of hours configuring become cluttered with AI features that don't respect how you actually work. Power users built intricate database relations, custom formulas, and automation chains in Notion precisely because it was flexible and predictable. Notion AI, for many of these users, feels like it was designed for casual note-takers, not the people who treat Notion like a second brain.

This isn't a story about AI being bad. It's about misaligned expectations, feature bloat, and the uncomfortable reality that the AI features many productivity tools are shipping in 2026 aren't keeping pace with what advanced users actually need. And it's driving a quiet migration to alternatives that either do AI better or don't pretend to do it at all.

What Happened: When Notion Added AI, Power Users Felt Left Behind

Notion launched its AI features in February 2023, initially as a limited alpha and then as a $10/month add-on to existing plans. The pitch was compelling: AI-powered writing assistance, automatic summaries, translation, and the ability to extract action items from meeting notes directly within your workspace. For casual users maintaining personal wikis or simple project lists, it worked well enough. You could highlight a paragraph and ask the AI to make it more professional or generate a blog post outline from bullet points.

But power users—the product managers running entire product backlogs through Notion databases, the consultants managing client projects with 20+ linked databases, the startup founders who've replaced half their software stack with Notion—quickly hit a wall. The AI couldn't understand context across databases. It couldn't reference properties from related pages. If you had a database of client projects linked to a database of meeting notes linked to a database of deliverables, Notion AI treated each page in isolation.

One specific pain point emerged repeatedly: asking Notion AI to summarize a project page that pulled data from multiple databases resulted in summaries that only looked at the text on that specific page, ignoring all the rich relational data that made Notion powerful in the first place. A project manager with a dashboard showing task completion rates, blockers, and team capacity would ask for a status summary and get back a rewrite of the page title and description—useless for someone who needs synthesis, not restatement.

The second frustration was speed and reliability. Power users working in large workspaces with hundreds of pages reported that Notion AI responses could take 8-12 seconds to generate, interrupting the flow of work in a tool that was supposed to enhance productivity. Compare this to using ChatGPT or Claude directly, where responses stream in near real-time, and the value proposition starts to crack.

By mid-2025, discussions in power user communities shifted from "how do you use Notion AI?" to "do you actually use Notion AI?" The answer, increasingly, was no. Users were paying for it because it came bundled with team plans or they'd forgotten to cancel, but they weren't integrating it into their workflows. The AI felt like it was designed by people who used Notion casually, not by people who'd built their entire operating system inside it.

The Concerns: Where Notion AI Falls Short for Advanced Workflows

The core complaint isn't that Notion AI is broken—it's that it's shallow. Power users don't need help rewriting sentences or generating generic summaries. They need AI that understands their workspace architecture. Imagine you're tracking 30 active projects across 5 clients, each with associated tasks, timelines, budgets, and meeting notes. What you want from AI is: "Which projects are at risk this week based on task completion velocity and upcoming deadlines?" or "Summarize all client feedback from the past month that relates to feature requests."

Notion AI can't answer these questions because it doesn't have workspace-level awareness. It operates at the page level, treating each page as an isolated document. This is fine for writing assistance but useless for knowledge synthesis. Power users have started calling it "expensive autocomplete" because that's essentially what it is—helpful for generating text, unhelpful for generating insights.

The second valid concern is cost structure. At $10/month per member, Notion AI becomes expensive quickly for teams. A 10-person team paying for Notion's Plus plan ($10/user) plus Notion AI ($10/user) is now spending $200/month. For that price, they could subscribe to ChatGPT Plus for key team members and get access to GPT-4, superior context windows, custom instructions, and the ability to upload and analyze documents directly. Many teams are making exactly this calculation.

The third issue is workflow interruption. Power users have muscle memory built around Notion's interface. Adding AI prompts and suggestions into that flow creates cognitive friction. One product designer described it as "pop-ups in my thought process"—AI suggestions appearing when she didn't want them, requiring an extra click to dismiss, breaking concentration. For users who've spent years optimizing their Notion workflows, any friction is unacceptable.

There's also a quality concern. Notion AI uses language models that, as of 2026, feel a generation behind what's available from OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google. Users report that summaries are often surface-level, missing nuance that GPT-4 or Claude would catch. When you're paying specifically for AI capability, using an inferior model feels like a bad deal.

Notion's Perspective: Building AI for the Majority, Not the Power Users

To be fair to Notion, they've been transparent that their AI features are designed for broad accessibility. In product communications, they've emphasized helping "everyone work smarter," which implicitly means focusing on the median user, not the 95th percentile. The median Notion user isn't running a 500-page workspace with 15 interconnected databases. They're managing a personal task list, a content calendar, or a small team wiki.

For those users, Notion AI genuinely adds value. Being able to draft a meeting agenda, translate notes, or generate ideas without leaving Notion reduces context switching and lowers the barrier to using AI. Notion has also argued that integrating AI directly into the workspace is more seamless than copying text to ChatGPT and back—a valid point for less technical users who aren't comfortable with API workflows or browser extensions.

Notion has continued to iterate, adding features like AI autofill for databases and Q&A across workspace content. These updates show awareness of power user needs, but execution has been slow and incomplete. As of mid-2026, the AI still doesn't reliably understand complex database relationships or provide the kind of deep synthesis that would justify the price premium.

What This Means for You: Should Power Users Stick With Notion?

If you're a power user currently paying for Notion AI, the honest recommendation is to evaluate whether you're actually using it for anything you can't do better elsewhere. Run this test: for one week, note every time you use Notion AI and ask yourself if you could have gotten a better result by pasting your content into ChatGPT, Claude, or even Grammarly. If the answer is consistently yes, you're paying for convenience that isn't actually convenient.

For power users who love Notion as a database and organization tool but need better AI, the emerging pattern is to use Notion for structure and external AI tools for intelligence. This might mean using Make.com to connect Notion databases to OpenAI's API for custom queries that understand your workspace architecture. Several power users have built automation workflows where they export database views to CSV, send them to GPT-4 via API, and get back analysis that Notion AI simply can't provide.

One consultant managing client projects described her setup: she uses Notion for all project tracking and documentation, but she's created a Make.com scenario that pulls her active projects database every Monday morning, sends it to GPT-4 with a custom prompt asking for risk analysis and priority recommendations, and posts the results back to a Notion page. This costs her about $3/month in API calls and gives her intelligence that Notion AI's $10/month subscription never could.

If you're not technical enough for API integrations, the simpler approach is just to cancel Notion AI and use ChatGPT or Claude in a separate tab when you need AI assistance. Yes, it's an extra step, but if the quality difference is significant, the friction is worth it. Power users care about output quality more than interface convenience.

For teams, the calculation is even clearer. Instead of paying $10/month per member for Notion AI, pay for 2-3 ChatGPT Plus subscriptions for the team members who need AI most, and have them share insights with the broader team. You'll save money and get better results.

The Bigger Picture: Why So Many Productivity Tools Are Getting AI Wrong

Notion isn't alone in this. Across the productivity software category, we're seeing the same pattern: tools that users already love adding AI features that feel half-baked, overpriced, or misaligned with how power users actually work. It's happening because AI has become a checkbox feature for SaaS companies afraid of being left behind. Investors want to see "AI strategy," marketing teams want to claim "AI-powered," and product teams are under pressure to ship something, even if it's not ready.

The problem is that bolting AI onto an existing product requires understanding not just what AI can do, but how it fits into established user workflows. Power users have spent years building systems that work. They're not looking for AI to replace those systems—they're looking for AI to make those systems smarter. That requires deep integration, contextual awareness, and respect for existing workflows. Most companies are shipping AI features that ignore all three.

We're also seeing a broader trend where the best AI experiences are happening outside the traditional productivity tools. Developers are using Cursor and Windsurf, which were built AI-first, rather than trying to make GitHub Copilot work inside their existing IDEs. Content creators are using dedicated AI writing tools like Sudowrite for fiction or NeuronWriter for SEO content rather than relying on Google Docs' AI suggestions. The pattern is clear: purpose-built AI tools are outperforming AI features bolted onto existing products.

This suggests that Notion's challenge isn't just execution—it's structural. Building truly intelligent AI for a flexible, database-driven workspace is genuinely hard. It might require rethinking Notion's architecture from the ground up, which isn't realistic for a company with millions of existing users and established workflows. This is why some power users are starting to wonder if Notion will ever be able to deliver the AI experience they want, or if they need to look elsewhere.

Worth Considering: Alternatives for Power Users

If you're frustrated enough with Notion AI that you're considering alternatives, here are the tools power users are actually migrating to:

  • Coda with AI: Similar flexibility to Notion with databases and documents combined, but with AI features that better understand document context and can reference across pages; power users report more useful automation and better API integration.
  • Airtable + ChatGPT API via Make.com: Use Airtable for database management and structure, then build custom AI workflows through Make.com that send your data to OpenAI's API for analysis; requires more setup but gives you complete control over prompts and AI behavior.
  • Obsidian + Local AI plugins: For users who want to own their data completely, Obsidian with plugins like Smart Connections or Text Generator gives you AI assistance without sending data to external servers; steeper learning curve but maximum flexibility and privacy.
  • ClickUp with Brain: ClickUp's AI feature, called Brain, is designed specifically for project management context and can answer questions across tasks, docs, and conversations in ways that feel more workspace-aware than Notion AI.

The honest truth is that none of these are perfect. Each involves trade-offs—more complexity, less polish, different limitations. But for power users frustrated with Notion AI's shallow integration, they're worth exploring. Several users are also adopting a hybrid approach: keeping Notion for what it does best (flexible databases and documentation) while using specialized tools for specific AI needs like Fireflies.ai for meeting intelligence or NeuronWriter for content optimization.

The Bottom Line: Notion Remains Great, But Its AI Isn't Why

Here's the balanced take: Notion is still an excellent tool for power users who need flexible databases, interconnected knowledge management, and customizable workflows. The core product hasn't gotten worse. If you built your system in Notion and it works, there's no urgent reason to migrate just because the AI features are disappointing.

But if you're currently paying for Notion AI, evaluate honestly whether it's delivering value proportional to its cost. For most power users, the answer is no. The AI features feel designed for a different user base—one that needs basic assistance with writing and summarization, not deep intelligence about complex workspace architectures.

What would change this assessment? Notion would need to ship AI that understands relational databases, can query across linked pages, and provides synthesis rather than just summarization. They'd need to match the quality of frontier models from OpenAI or Anthropic, or at least get close enough that the convenience of integration justifies the quality gap. And they'd need to do it at a price point that makes sense compared to using ChatGPT Plus or Claude Pro directly.

Until then, the smartest approach for power users is to use Notion for structure and better AI tools for intelligence. Keep your workspace, cancel the AI add-on, and spend that $10/month on a ChatGPT subscription instead. Your databases will be just as powerful, your workflows just as smooth, and your AI assistance significantly better. That's not the outcome Notion wants, but it's the reality power users are living in June 2026.
